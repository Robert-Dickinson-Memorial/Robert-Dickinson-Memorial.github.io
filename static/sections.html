His life

A curious mind. A generous spirit.

The story of a scientist who kept widening the questions he asked—and the circle of people he welcomed into them.

1940–2026

Robert Earl Dickinson helped change how humanity understands the living Earth.

Born in Millersburg, Ohio, and raised in Minnesota, Robert carried an expansive curiosity into a lifetime of science. He studied chemistry and physics at Harvard University, graduating in 1961, then turned to meteorology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, earning his master’s degree in 1962 and Ph.D. in 1966.

He joined the National Center for Atmospheric Research in 1968. Early in his career, he advanced understanding of how planetary waves transfer energy through the atmosphere. Later, as a leader in NCAR’s Climate and Global Dynamics Division, he confronted a central weakness in the era’s climate models: land was treated largely as a passive store of water. Robert helped recast it as a dynamic system of soils, plants, water, energy, and carbon.

That insight reshaped global climate modeling. His pioneering work brought vegetation and land-surface processes into climate models and helped establish the intellectual foundations of modern Earth-system science. Across five decades, his research connected atmospheric dynamics with hydrology, drought, remote sensing, aerosols, tropical deforestation, and the terrestrial carbon cycle.

Robert held professorships at the University of Arizona and Georgia Tech before joining The University of Texas at Austin in 2008. At UT’s Jackson School of Geosciences, he was known not only as a giant of climate science, but as a patient and exacting mentor.

His deepest legacy lives in both the models that now describe a more complete Earth and the people he trained to ask better questions of it.

01

Harvard & MIT · 1961–1968

Finding order in planetary-scale motion

ScalePlanetary waves

After studying chemistry and physics at Harvard, Robert turned to meteorology at MIT. His earliest work used fluid dynamics to explain how vast waves form, propagate, and exchange energy in rotating atmospheres.

What he advanced

  • Developed theoretical approaches to Rossby waves, zonal flows, and large-scale atmospheric disturbances.
  • Connected rigorous mathematics with the physical behavior of atmospheres and other rotating systems.
  • Established a way of thinking that later carried from individual motions to the climate system as a whole.

Why it mattered

He began by asking how motion travels through an atmosphere—the foundation for a career spent connecting ever larger parts of the Earth system.
Atmospheric dynamicsPlanetary wavesTheory
02

NCAR · 1968–1990

Expanding from atmospheric theory to climate

ScaleAtmosphere to climate

At NCAR, Robert’s science widened from upper-atmospheric dynamics and planetary atmospheres to global circulation and climate. As a scientist, section head, and deputy director, he helped shape both the models and the community building them.

What he advanced

  • Advanced understanding of circulation, radiation, and the upper atmospheres of Earth, Venus, and Mars.
  • Helped establish climate modeling as a framework for studying interacting global processes.
  • Pioneered land-surface representations that brought soil, water, and vegetation into global climate models.

Why it mattered

Land was no longer merely the lower boundary of an atmospheric model. It became an active, living participant in climate.

03

University of Arizona · 1990–1999

Making the living land visible to climate models

ScaleLand–atmosphere system

Working across atmospheric science, hydrology, and tree-ring research, Robert deepened the physical description of the land surface and helped make those models testable against observations.

What he advanced

  • Linked vegetation, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, snow, and surface energy exchange.
  • Advanced model evaluation through land-surface intercomparison and field observations.
  • Connected satellite measurements and canopy reflectance with drought, hydrology, and tropical deforestation.

Why it mattered

His work helped turn land modeling into an observational science that could be compared, challenged, and improved.
HydrologyRemote sensingDeforestationModel evaluation
04

Georgia Tech · 1999–2008

Connecting the exchanges that make an Earth system

ScaleWater, energy, and carbon

At Georgia Tech, Robert brought atmospheric physics, hydrology, ecosystems, and biogeochemistry into closer conversation, treating climate as a coupled Earth system.

What he advanced

  • Advanced land models connecting roots, soil moisture, surface energy, ecosystems, and carbon.
  • Used remote sensing and observations to test land temperature, vegetation, and exchange.
  • Extended his leadership globally through the IPCC, AGU, and an international group of students and collaborators.

Why it mattered

The question expanded to how water, energy, carbon, vegetation, and atmosphere continually reshape one another.
Earth-system modelingCarbon cycleHydrologyScientific leadership
05

UT Austin · 2008–2018

Synthesis—and a new generation of scientists

ScaleCoupled Earth system

At UT Austin, Robert continued to connect models with observations while giving unusual care to students, postdoctoral scholars, and visiting scientists. From 2018, he continued as a Distinguished Professor in Residence at UCLA.

What he advanced

  • Studied drought, soil-moisture feedbacks, vegetation, surface temperature, circulation, and extremes.
  • Helped frame national priorities for climate modeling, prediction, and sustained satellite observations.
  • Mentored researchers across disciplines and continents, often working beside them in models and code.

Why it mattered

His legacy lived in both a more complete representation of Earth and a worldwide community trained to keep improving it.
