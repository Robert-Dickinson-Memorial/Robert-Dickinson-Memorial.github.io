Robert Earl Dickinson helped change how humanity understands the living Earth.

Born in Millersburg, Ohio, and raised in Minnesota, Robert carried an expansive curiosity into a lifetime of science. He studied chemistry and physics at Harvard University, graduating in 1961, then turned to meteorology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, earning his master’s degree in 1962 and Ph.D. in 1966.

Robert was known as a patient and exacting mentor. He gave care and attention to students, postdoctoral scholars, and visiting scientists, and valued the people he worked with as much as the questions they explored together.

His career took him from MIT and NCAR to the University of Arizona, Georgia Tech, UT Austin, and UCLA. After retiring from UT Austin, he remained active in collaboration and mentorship. Curiosity, physical insight, and generosity continued to shape his conversations with colleagues across generations.

01

MIT · 1961–1968

Finding order in planetary-scale motion

ScalePlanetary waves

How do planetary-scale waves move through the atmosphere and interact with its circulation? Robert showed why Earth’s spherical geometry matters for wave propagation, and how atmospheric waveguides direct energy and momentum toward critical lines where they are absorbed.

Key contributions

  • Advanced the theory of vertically propagating planetary and Rossby waves in a spherical atmosphere.
  • Developed fundamental ideas about waveguides, critical-line absorption, and wave–mean-flow interaction.
  • Helped lay the conceptual foundation for modern understanding of stratospheric dynamics and thermodynamics; his early work foreshadowed diagnostic tools still used today.

Legacy

This was the beginning of Bob’s characteristic scientific style: identify a fundamental physical problem, strip it to its essentials, and then follow its implications across scales.

Landmark publication · 1968

Planetary Rossby Waves Propagating Vertically Through Weak Westerly Wind Wave Guides

Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences, 25, 984–1002

This landmark paper developed the first theory of Rossby-wave critical-line absorption, extended the atmospheric refractive-index concept into two dimensions, and introduced a group-velocity framework that anticipated the Eliassen–Palm flux vector.

02

NCAR · 1968–1990

Expanding from atmospheric theory to climate

ScaleAtmosphere to climate

Robert connected atmospheric circulation and radiation with the problem of climate change. His work helped make climate models a way to investigate interacting physical processes, while bringing soils, water, and vegetation into their representation of land.

What he advanced

  • Advanced understanding of circulation, radiation, and the upper atmospheres of Earth, Venus, and Mars.
  • Helped establish climate modeling as a framework for studying interacting global processes.
  • Pioneered land-surface representations that brought soil, water, and vegetation into global climate models.

Why it mattered

Land was no longer merely the lower boundary of an atmospheric model. It became an active, living participant in climate.

03

University of Arizona · 1990–1999

Making the living land visible to climate models

ScaleLand–atmosphere system

Robert deepened the physical description of the land surface and helped make land models testable against observations. Vegetation, soil moisture, and surface energy exchange became processes that could be measured, compared, and improved.

What he advanced

  • Linked vegetation, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, snow, and surface energy exchange.
  • Advanced model evaluation through land-surface intercomparison and field observations.
  • Connected satellite measurements and canopy reflectance with drought, hydrology, and tropical deforestation.

Why it mattered

His work helped turn land modeling into an observational science that could be compared, challenged, and improved.
HydrologyRemote sensingDeforestationModel evaluation
04

Georgia Tech · 1999–2008

Connecting the exchanges that make an Earth system

ScaleWater, energy, and carbon

Robert brought atmospheric physics, hydrology, ecosystems, biogeochemistry, and satellite observations together to study climate as a coupled Earth system. The question became how exchanges of water, energy, and carbon shape one another.

What he advanced

  • Advanced land models connecting roots, soil moisture, surface energy, ecosystems, and carbon.
  • Used remote sensing and observations to test land temperature, vegetation, and exchange.
  • Extended his leadership globally through the IPCC, AGU, and an international group of students and collaborators.

Why it mattered

The question expanded to how water, energy, carbon, vegetation, and atmosphere continually reshape one another.
Earth-system modelingCarbon cycleHydrologyScientific leadership
05

UT Austin · 2008–2018

Synthesis—and a new generation of scientists

ScaleCoupled Earth system

Robert used models and observations together to study drought, vegetation stress, soil-moisture feedbacks, and climate extremes. Satellite measurements offered a way to test whether models captured the behavior of the living land.

What he advanced

  • Studied drought, soil-moisture feedbacks, vegetation, surface temperature, circulation, and extremes.
  • Helped frame national priorities for climate modeling, prediction, and sustained satellite observations.
  • Mentored researchers across disciplines and continents, often working beside them in models and code.

Why it mattered

His legacy lived in both a more complete representation of Earth and a worldwide community trained to keep improving it.

UCLA · 2018–2026

Keeping the scientific conversation alive

Scientific focusSynthesis and mentorship

Robert continued to connect ideas across atmospheric science and Earth-system modeling, returning to first principles as new models and observations emerged. Collaboration and mentorship carried that approach into the work of younger scientists.

Key contributions

  • Continued collaborating across atmospheric science, climate, land-surface processes, and Earth-system modeling.
  • Shared decades of physical insight with students and colleagues working on new generations of models and observations.
  • Sustained an international scientific community built as much through generosity and conversation as through publications.

Legacy

His final professional chapter made clear that his enduring contribution was not only a body of science, but a way of doing science—curious, physical, collaborative, and generous.

Community service

Serving the scientific community

Robert helped shape the institutions, collaborations, and journals through which Earth science advances.

  • Leading scientific societies

    He served as President of the American Geophysical Union (2002–2004), following his presidency of its Atmospheric Sciences Section (1988–1992). He also chaired the AAAS Section on Atmospheric and Hydrospheric Sciences (2001–2002).

  • Bringing science to national decisions

    As chair of the National Research Council’s Climate Research Committee (1990–1992) and a member of numerous later committees, he advised on climate modeling, prediction, global change research, and continuity of satellite observations. He also served on the UCAR Board of Trustees (2006–2009).

  • Connecting international research

    Robert was a Lead Author of Chapter 7 of the IPCC Fourth Assessment Report (2004–2007). He co-chaired the IGBP–IHDP–WCRP Joint Carbon Project Committee (2001–2006) and U.S. CLIVAR (2001–2002), helping connect research across disciplines and institutions.

  • Stewarding scientific publishing

    His editorial service included Editor of the Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences (1991–1993), Associate Editor of the Journal of Climate (1993–1995), and Editor-in-Chief of Carbon Balance and Management (2005–2010). He also served on the PNAS Editorial Board.

Sources: Robert E. Dickinson’s curriculum vitae; foreword to the 2020 AMS Robert E. Dickinson Symposium, prepared by Xubin Zeng, Leo Donner, Ricky Rood, and Alan Robock.
